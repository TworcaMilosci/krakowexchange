## Three costs hidden in one withdrawal

What you really pay for a cash withdrawal abroad is made of three separate things. It helps to keep them apart, because you control each one differently.

| Cost | Who sets it | What you can do |
|---|---|---|
| Your bank's fee for a foreign withdrawal and for converting the currency | The bank that issued your card | Check the terms before you travel, or bring a different card |
| The ATM's own withdrawal fee | The ATM operator | Read the screen; if a fee appears, cancel and try another machine |
| Currency conversion at the ATM (DCC) | The ATM operator | Decline it. Always |

You cannot change the first cost at the machine, because it comes from your agreement with your bank. The second depends on the machine and on your card: the same ATM may charge one card and not another. The third is entirely optional and usually the most expensive, so it is the one to get right.

## Which ATM to use

We do not publish a list of "good" and "bad" networks. Fees for foreign cards change, they depend on who issued your card, and the lists you find online contradict one another. A few rules that you can check on the spot in half a minute work better.

1. **Look for an ATM at a bank branch.** Ideally one set into the wall of the bank's own building or in its lobby. If the machine keeps your card or does not pay out, there is someone on site who can help, especially during branch hours. Bank ATMs tend to add their own fee to foreign cards less often, but there is no rule without exceptions.
2. **Be careful with standalone machines in tourist spots**: at shop entrances, in passages, at airports and stations. Not every one is expensive, but this is where you are most likely to meet both a withdrawal fee and a heavily promoted conversion offer.
3. **Read each screen to the end.** If an ATM charges its own fee, it tells you and asks for your consent before paying out. That is the moment to press "cancel", take your card and walk on. In the centre of Kraków the next ATM is usually a few dozen metres away.
4. **Type your own amount.** Some machines open with preset, high amounts. Choose "other amount" and enter what you actually need.
5. **Withdraw less often, but sensibly.** If your bank charges a flat fee per withdrawal, one larger withdrawal costs less than three small ones. Do not take more than you will spend, though. Our guide on [how much money you need for a weekend in Kraków](/knowledge-base/how-much-money-weekend-in-krakow) gives realistic numbers.
6. **Bring the physical card.** Paying by phone works very well in Poland, but contactless cash withdrawals with a foreign phone wallet are not supported everywhere.

## How to decline the conversion, step by step

The order of screens differs between machines, but the pattern is similar.

1. Insert your card, choose English and enter your PIN.
2. Choose a withdrawal and an amount in złoty.
3. If the ATM charges its own fee, a screen shows the amount. Accept it or cancel.
4. The **conversion screen** appears. It shows the amount in your home currency, a rate, and phrases such as "guaranteed rate", "know exactly what you pay" or "no surprises". There are two buttons: one accepts the conversion, the other declines it.
5. Press **"decline conversion"**, "without conversion", "continue in PLN" or similar wording.
6. The ATM pays out złoty. Your account is charged in PLN, and your bank or card network converts it to your currency.

Two things confuse people most. First, the accept button is often larger and brighter, and declining can look like cancelling the whole withdrawal. It is not: you are declining the conversion only. Second, some screens ask twice whether you really want to "risk an unknown rate". The answer stays the same.

The Polish wording you may see is *bez przewalutowania* (without conversion) or *odrzuć przewalutowanie* (decline conversion). Card terminals in shops and restaurants ask the same question in a different form. We explain that case in [Pay in PLN or your home currency?](/knowledge-base/pay-in-pln-or-home-currency-dcc-krakow)

## What the rules say

EU rules on cross-border payments require a party offering currency conversion at an ATM or card terminal to show the total cost before the transaction starts, as a percentage markup over the European Central Bank reference rate. It must also tell you that you can pay in the local currency and let your own card provider convert. This obligation has applied since 19 April 2020 (Regulation (EU) 2019/518, checked November 2026).

In practice this is a line of small print on the conversion screen. The rules cover payments in euros and in the national currencies of EU countries, so with a card in pounds or US dollars you will usually see just the offered rate and the final amount. Whatever your currency, one thing does not change: the conversion is an offer, not a condition of getting your cash.

## Staying safe at the ATM

Kraków is a calm city, and the habits are the same as anywhere.

- Cover the keypad with your hand when you enter your PIN.
- If the card slot or keypad looks loose or stuck on, use another machine.
- Do not accept "help" from strangers at an ATM, and do not let anyone stand right behind you.
- Put the cash away before you step back. Counting notes in the street is not a good idea.
- If the machine keeps your card or does not pay out, note the time and place and call your bank. This is why an ATM at an open branch is the more comfortable choice.
- Turn on transaction notifications in your banking app. You will see at once in which currency and for how much you were charged.

If someone approaches you in the street offering to change money, walk on. Typical situations are described in our guide on [avoiding exchange scams in Kraków](/avoid-currency-exchange-scams-krakow).

## ATM or exchange office: when each makes sense

There is no single answer for everyone. It depends on what you arrive with.

**You have a card only.** Withdraw from an ATM with the conversion declined, and pay by card in PLN in shops and restaurants. A walk-in exchange office cannot turn a card balance into cash.

**You have cash in your own currency.** Then you do not need an ATM. Exchanging at an exchange office (in Polish: *kantor*) has one advantage: the whole cost is visible in advance. It is the gap between the buy and sell rate on the board, with no commission and nothing added later.
