**In short:** Poland's currency is the złoty (PLN), and you cannot count on paying in dollars. If you are bringing US cash, exchange it in Kraków, not at home or at the airport. Bring clean, recent bills, look at the **buy** rate next to USD, compare it with the mid-market rate on your phone, and ask how many złoty you will receive before you hand over your notes. Exchange Kantor Black Sheep at Floriańska 37 buys and sells US dollars with 0% commission and a retail spread of up to 0.09 PLN.

The US dollar is one of the three currencies we exchange every day, alongside the euro and the British pound. It arrives with visitors from the United States, with travellers from other continents who carry dollars as their universal cash, and with people visiting family in Poland. With dollars, most questions at the counter are not about the rate but about the bills themselves, so that is where this guide spends the most time.

## Can you pay in dollars in Kraków?

Expect to pay in złoty. Prices in shops, restaurants, museums and on public transport are in PLN. Some businesses on the tourist routes may take dollar bills, but they set their own rate and usually give change in złoty, so it is rarely a good deal.

Cards and phones work almost everywhere. For those payments the only rule is to choose PLN when the terminal asks which currency to charge; the reason is explained in our guide to [paying in PLN and dynamic currency conversion](/knowledge-base/pay-in-pln-or-home-currency-dcc-krakow). Cash is still handy for market stalls, tips, small bakeries, public toilets and the occasional "cash only" bar. Our guide to [the cost of a weekend in Kraków](/knowledge-base/how-much-money-weekend-in-krakow) suggests how much to carry.

## Exchange at home or in Kraków?

Exchange offices earn their money on the gap between the rate at which they buy a currency and the rate at which they sell it. That gap, the spread, is narrow for currencies an office trades all day and wide for currencies it rarely sees.

In the United States the złoty is a minor currency. Where you can find it at all, the spread tends to be wide. In Kraków it is the other way round: dollars arrive every day, offices compete for them, and you can compare several boards within a few minutes' walk. Airports are the exception in every country. They rarely compete on the rate, so if you land with no złoty, pay for the train or taxi into town by card and exchange cash in the centre.

## How to read the USD rate

Every board shows two numbers next to the dollar.

- **Buy (kupno, "we buy")**: the office buys dollars from you. This is your rate when you arrive with dollars.
- **Sell (sprzedaż, "we sell")**: the office sells dollars to you. This applies when you change leftover złoty back.

The buy rate is always the lower one. An example with made-up numbers, not today's rates: if the board shows USD 3.90 / 3.99, then for $200 you receive 200 × 3.90 = 780 złoty. The difference between the two numbers, here 0.09 PLN, is the spread. Our current rates are always on the [home page](/#rates), and the full explanation is in the guide to [buy and sell rates](/knowledge-base/buy-rate-vs-sell-rate-kantor).

## What a fair offer looks like

The spread tells you more about an office than any slogan in the window. The numbers below are examples.

| | Buys USD at | Sells USD at | Spread | For $500 you get |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Office with a narrow spread | 3.90 | 3.99 | 0.09 PLN | 1,950 PLN |
| Outlet with a wide spread | 3.55 | 4.25 | 0.70 PLN | 1,775 PLN |

That is 175 PLN lost on one exchange, and both places can truthfully display "0% commission", because commission and rate are two separate things.

A one-minute check that works anywhere in Poland:

1. Search "USD PLN" on your phone to see the mid-market rate.
2. A fair buy rate is a few groszy below that number (one złoty is 100 groszy). Tens of groszy below means keep walking.
3. Make sure the board shows **both** rates for USD, and that the number next to the $ sign really is the US dollar and not the Canadian or Australian one.
4. Ask: "I have 200 dollars. How many złoty will I get?" Hand over your bills only after you hear the answer.

## Which dollar bills to bring

At home a dollar is a dollar. Abroad, the condition and age of a bill matter more, because an exchange office has to be able to verify it and pass it on. A little sorting before you fly saves time at the counter.

- **Bring recent designs.** The current $100 with the blue 3-D security ribbon has been issued since 2013. The $20, $50, $10 and $5 bills with subtle background colours date from 2003, 2004, 2006 and 2008. These are exchanged without questions.
- **Bring clean, flat bills.** A fold or a wallet crease is fine. Tears, tape, ink stamps, pen marks and bills that went through the wash are a different matter.
- **$50 and $100 bills are fine here.** Unlike in some shops at home, large denominations are everyday business for an exchange office. Bring a few $20s as well if you want to change a little at a time.
- **Leave coins at home.** We do not exchange US coins, and neither do most offices abroad.

All bills are the same size whatever their value, so count by denomination, both when you hand them over and when you get any back.

## Older series: valid, but ask first

The United States has never cancelled its banknotes. According to the U.S. Currency Education Program, all US currency issued since 1861 remains valid and redeemable at full face value, and nobody is required to trade old designs for new ones.

Outside the United States, practice is more cautious. Designs from before the 1996 redesign have fewer security features and are harder to check, so some exchange offices and banks abroad are reluctant to take them, or refuse them altogether. That is a matter of caution, not of the bill's validity.

For reference, the redesign with larger portraits started with the $100 in 1996, followed by the $50 in 1997, the $20 in 1998, and the $10 and $5 in 2000. The series year is printed on the front of each bill, next to the portrait.

If older bills are all you have, call us or send a photo on WhatsApp at +48 889 505 057 before you come. We will tell you straight away whether and on what terms we can take them. If you are still at home, the simplest fix is to swap them for current bills at your own bank before you travel.

## Damaged dollar bills

Torn, taped, stained, washed or written-on notes can be a problem at any exchange office. In the United States, badly damaged currency is handled by the Bureau of Engraving and Printing, and under US regulations full value is paid, as a rule, when clearly more than 50% of a note is present and identifiable. That process is designed for people in the US, so it is easier to deal with a damaged bill at home than from a hotel room in Kraków.

We buy damaged banknotes only after you contact us first. Each note is assessed individually, so send a photo before you walk over. Details are in our guide to [damaged and withdrawn banknotes](/exchange-damaged-withdrawn-banknotes-krakow). Please do not tape a torn bill yourself; bring the pieces as they are.

## Changing złoty back to dollars

Leftover złoty can be changed back before you leave, because we sell dollars as well as buy them. This time the **sell** rate applies, the higher of the two numbers. Since you pay the spread once in each direction, the cheapest approach is not to exchange more than you expect to spend. Polish coins cannot be exchanged, so spend them on a last coffee or keep them for the next trip. If you fly out on a Sunday or early on Monday, remember that we are closed on Sunday; Saturday until 20:30 is the last convenient moment.

## Things to avoid

1. **Airport and hotel desks** for anything more than pocket money.
2. **ATMs that offer to charge your card in dollars.** Decline the conversion and take the cash in PLN.
3. **A window with one attractive number**, or a board that shows no rates at all. Ask for the amount in złoty first. More examples are in our guide on [avoiding exchange scams in Kraków](/avoid-currency-exchange-scams-krakow).
4. **Paying in dollar bills in shops and restaurants.** The rate is whatever the business decides.
5. **Exchanging everything on day one.** Change what you need and keep the rest in dollars.
